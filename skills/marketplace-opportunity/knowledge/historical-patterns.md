# Six historical regularities

From Bronze Age Kanesh to Amazon, marketplaces have never been spontaneous or ungoverned. The full evidence is in `docs/research/history-of-marketplaces.md`.

1. **Someone always grants the franchise and takes a toll.** Historically it was city-states, royal charters (three-quarters of English markets to 1516 came by royal grant), lords and councils. Today it is regulators designating gatekeepers after the fact, plus app stores and AI assistants acting as gatekeepers of discovery.
   *Ask:* who controls permission to trade or to be found in this niche, and what do they take?

2. **Liquidity is engineered in time and space.** Market days, rotating fairs, catchment rules (Bracton's 6⅔-mile radius) and caravanserais a day's journey apart all did this. Constrained launches and atomic networks solve the same thickness problem.
   *Ask:* how can trade be concentrated in time and place to reach thickness cheaply?

3. **Trust is always hybrid.** Reputation works inside public or publicly backed enforcement: market officials (*agoranomoi*), market courts (piepowder courts), market inspectors (*muhtasib*), fair-wardens, market queens. The "private order" story (the Maghribi traders, the law merchant) has been badly damaged by archival work.
   *Ask:* what public enforcement will this platform sit inside, and what will regulators expect of it?

4. **The law has flipped from forestalling to anti-steering.** Medieval law made dealing outside the market a crime. Modern law (the DMA, the Epic cases, CMA steering rules) stops platforms blocking off-platform deals.
   *Ask:* does the leakage defence depend on blocking contact? If so, it is legally fragile.

5. **Place gives way to process, but physical place finds new jobs.** Abstraction keeps advancing, yet physical markets survive as experience, provenance and sociability. Digital marketplaces are re-acquiring physical place through warehouses, pick-up and drop-off networks and dark stores.
   *Ask:* is there a physical or logistics layer that creates control?

6. **Incumbents close markets; rulers open them when it pays.** Examples: guilds and Japan's free-market edicts (*rakuichi rakuza*, from 1549), fixed commissions and London's Big Bang (1986), platform exclusivity and the DMA.
   *Ask:* is an incumbent's closed position about to be forced open, creating an opening?

## Base rates to remember

- **Failure is normal.** Only 39% of English places holding a market or market grant in 1300 were still market towns around 1600 (Letters, *Gazetteer of Markets and Fairs*). The modern playbook, built from survivors, has no comparable failure count.
- **Direct precedent for self-preferencing.** SABRE's bias in favour of its owner's flights was banned in 1984; Google was fined €460m under the DMA in 2026 for favouring its own results. The same conduct drew the same remedy logic.
