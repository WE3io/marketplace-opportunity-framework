# Whoever grants the market collects the toll

Across four millennia of written evidence, from Old Assyrian merchant colonies to Amazon, marketplaces have never been spontaneous or ungoverned. A market exists because some authority grants the right to hold it, decides where and when trade may happen, polices weights, quality and disputes, and takes a cut. What changes is who holds that authority (city-state, emperor, lord, municipal council, exchange club, private platform) and how far the market has drifted from a bounded place and day towards a placeless process. The question that dominated twentieth-century scholarship, whether pre-modern societies had "real" markets, is largely settled. Price-responsive exchange, credit and commercial law existed in the Bronze Age Near East, Classical Greece and Rome (high confidence), and state-regulated marketplaces are well attested in imperial China (walled official markets by Tang times) and Aztec Mesoamerica. How integrated and growth-generating those markets were is still disputed, and the share of any ancient economy that passed through markets is effectively unknown. Trust has always rested on merchant reputation working inside public enforcement. The "private order" account of medieval trade, much cited as a precedent for eBay-style reputation systems, has been badly damaged by recent archival work (moderate–high confidence). Digital platforms put into one private firm functions that history usually spread across several holders: franchise holder, rule-maker, court, toll-collector and often the largest trader. They do this at global scale. The structural parallels with chartered markets hold well. The "technofeudal" analogy holds poorly. Today's regulatory response inverts the medieval legal logic: medieval law forced trade into the marketplace, whereas today's regulators deputise platforms as inspectors and tax collectors and ban them from blocking off-platform dealing. For anyone building or regulating a marketplace, the long record says the decisive variables are governance, trust infrastructure and the toll, not the technology.

*How to read this report.* The confidence labels (high, moderate, low) attach to disputable empirical claims. "Contested" or "experts disagree" means published specialists take opposing positions. "Unknown" means the evidence base does not answer the question. "Not covered" means the research behind this report did not reach the topic, which is a gap in this report, not in the scholarship. Passages marked "synthesis" are my interpretation, not the cited authors'. Claims the underlying research could not verify have been left out or are marked unverified. Several dates for Islamic, Asian, African and nineteenth-century institutions rest on tertiary sources (chiefly Wikipedia), and their confidence levels reflect that.

## Bronze Age merchants charged 30% interest without a market square

The oldest evidence is of exchange, not of marketplaces. Obsidian from central and eastern Anatolia reached distant Near Eastern sites from the start of the Neolithic, c. 10,000–7000 BCE. Network modelling now favours "small-world" links between well-supplied villages over Colin Renfrew's older model of obsidian passed "down the line" from neighbour to neighbour ([Ibáñez et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4590507)). This shows networks existed, but it says nothing about how prices were set. Confidence is high that the exchange happened and low–moderate on how it was organised. The first textual hint of a market concept comes from Ebla, c. 2400–2350 BCE, where M.G. Biga reads the term KI.LAM as a noun meaning "market, fair" (moderate) ([Rubio, BMCR 2005](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005-02-02.html)).

Mesopotamia then poses the puzzle that runs through this whole history: sophisticated commerce with little sign of a central market square. Exchange clustered at the *kārum*, a word meaning quay, port and commercial district, used for both the place and the institution ([Rubio](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005-02-02.html)). A. Leo Oppenheim stressed that Mesopotamian cities had no central marketplace. W.F. Leemans countered that "the kar was the market, i.e. locus where prices were formed and merchants met". Gareth Dale's re-appraisal concludes that Oppenheim "was not wrong" about the marginal role of marketplaces in southern Mesopotamian cities ([Dale](https://core.ac.uk/download/29140805.pdf)). The defensible description is **market exchange without a marketplace**. Confidence is moderate, because experts disagree on whether the *kārum* was where prices were formed.

The best-documented case is the trade between Assur and Kanesh (Kültepe) in Anatolia, c. 2000–1700 BCE. It has yielded about **23,000 texts**, mostly legal and commercial, and has been called the "first attested commercial society" ([NY Journal of Books on Larsen](https://nyjournalofbooks.com/book-review/ancient-kanesh)). Private family firms from Assur traded there under the protection of the Assur state, which Klaas Veenhof calls "a highly protectionist system" ([Rubio](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005-02-02.html)). Jan Gerrit Dercksen's 2022 synthesis records the earliest known interest rate, **33⅓% (c. 1931 BCE)**, and a standard rate of **30% a year from c. 1906 BCE**. Net trading profits ran at about 50%, and joint-stock funds (*naruqqum*) lasted ten years or more. Dozens of colonies and stations lined the route under treaties with local rulers. Assur's city hall blocked Babylonian rivals from crossing the Euphrates into Anatolia and collected levies, such as ten pounds of silver from Kanesh towards Assur's city wall ([Dercksen 2022](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:3721454/download)).

Specialists diverge on volume. Estimates of annual flows differ several-fold, from Dercksen's working minimum of about 1,000 donkey-loads a year to Stratford's estimate of up to 5,000 ([Dercksen](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:3721454/download)). That is a case of experts disagreeing, not of missing evidence. The archive is rich enough for economists to estimate a structural gravity model of Bronze Age trade. It finds that large cities arose where natural routes crossed and that city-size patterns persisted for four millennia ([Barjamovic et al., NBER/QJE](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23992)). The governance lesson is present from the start: profit-seeking private firms operated inside a framework of treaties, colonies, protection and levies set by a city-state.

Price series arrive in the first millennium BCE. The Babylonian Astronomical Diaries, kept c. 652–61 BCE, record the market values in Babylon of six commodities: barley, dates, mustard, cress, sesame and wool ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomical_diaries)). Prices moved with harvests and shocks, so they were not fixed by administrators. But R.J. van der Spek and Bas van Leeuwen find Babylonian staple prices more volatile than in most Mediterranean regions and less correlated with Rome. Barley and date price shocks took between 9.5 months and 3.5 years to work through ([van der Spek & van Leeuwen 2013](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucg:wpaper:0047)). Babylon is therefore evidence both for markets and for their limits (moderate–high). Further east, Harappan cubical weights followed a single binary-then-decimal standard across settlements ([Harappa.com](https://www.harappa.com/blog/cubical-weights)). Standardised measurement is necessary for market exchange but does not prove it. Pharaonic and Roman Egypt, whose papyri are among antiquity's richest price sources, was not covered.

### The "no markets" orthodoxy collapsed, but the integration question did not

Twentieth-century debate turned on Karl Polanyi and Moses Finley. Polanyi's *Trade and Market* (1957) included the essay "Marketless Trading in Hammurabi's Time". It argued that in non-market trade, supply depends more on security and politics than on price ([Rotstein 1970](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/karl-polanyis-concept-of-nonmarket-trade/966940FC09777E8E049899CE1477D5A3)). Polanyi presented the "port of trade", a neutral enclave separating trade from market, as a recurring pattern, from Whydah to Aztec and Maya centres and early-medieval emporia ([Polanyi 1963](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/ports-of-trade-in-early-societies/8D1B00BB5097A0962655EAD08141264E)). Finley's *The Ancient Economy* (1973) argued that Greek and Roman economic life was embedded in status and centred on the household, with no distinct economy responding to market forces ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ancient_Economy)). Finley himself rejected the "primitivist" label usually attached to him ([BMCR 2009.06.51](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=26542)).

The strong version of this orthodoxy has not survived. Dale judges Polanyi's "absolute thesis" refuted: that Babylon had "neither market places nor a functioning market system of any description". His "qualified thesis", that Babylonian trade was not originally market activity, holds up largely for subsistence goods. Veenhof, Larsen and Dercksen have rejected Polanyi's portrait of Old Assyrian merchants as salaried quasi-officials ([Dale](https://core.ac.uk/download/29140805.pdf)). On Finley, the introduction to a 2016 Cambridge volume on his legacy reports that scholars spent a generation trying to substantiate his claims before concluding the task was "vain" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ancient_Economy)). In Mesoamerica, Kenneth Hirth argues the Aztecs built "one of the most sophisticated market economies in the ancient world" without navigable rivers or draught animals ([Hirth 2016](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-aztec-economic-world-merchants-and-markets-in-ancient-mesoame/)). That undercuts Polanyi's reading of Aztec centres as ports of trade (moderate).

What remains contested is scale and integration. Peter Temin argues that markets dominated the Roman economy. He describes "a reasonably vibrant market for wheat" across the empire and claims second-century Italy was as prosperous as the seventeenth-century Dutch Republic ([Temin 2013](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781400845422/html)). Peter Fibiger Bang's *The Roman Bazaar* (2008) finds "very little evidence for integrated markets". He sees volatile prices, compartmentalised merchant networks and a predatory, fiscally driven state ([Kiiskinen, BMCR](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=26542)). Within the *Cambridge Economic History of the Greco-Roman World*, Walter Scheidel sees "a low equilibrium trap" and Willem Jongman sees "unprecedented levels of prosperity" ([Katsari, BMCR](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2009/2009.04.74)).

Part of the dispute is about definitions. Polanyi and Finley denied a *self-regulating market system* in which land, labour and capital were commodities. Their critics mostly demonstrate that *markets* existed. As Mayhew, Neale and Tandy objected to Morris Silver in 1985, specialisation alone does not prove that markets existed ([Mayhew et al.](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/markets-in-the-ancient-near-east-a-challenge-to-silvers-argument-and-use-of-evidence/418EA713D80767E6A9DA763030411519)). The most important missing number is what share of any ancient economy passed through markets. No source reviewed provides it, so it is best treated as unknown.

| Claim about ancient economies | Status | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Price-responsive market exchange existed in the Bronze Age Near East | Established (Kanesh archive, Babylonian price series) | High |
| Mesopotamian cities had a central marketplace like a medieval square | Majority view says no; Leemans-type dissent | Moderate (that they did not) |
| Polanyi's "marketless Babylon" | Refuted | High |
| Ancient economies were embedded in social and political institutions | Survives, recast in institutional-economics terms | Moderate–high |

## Classical states policed weights and conduct, and sometimes traded themselves

The Greek agora combined civic and commercial life. The word meant a public assembly and also the place where assemblies and markets were held ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/agora)). Athens regulated commerce through boards of officials chosen by lot. The Aristotelian *Constitution of the Athenians* (c. 330s–320s BCE) lists five *agoranomoi* for the city and five for Piraeus to ensure goods were "pure and unadulterated", and ten *metronomoi* to check weights and measures. *Sitophylakes*, eventually 20 for the city and 15 for Piraeus, ensured grain sold at "reasonable prices" and fixed the weight of loaves. Ten superintendents of the *emporion* compelled merchants to bring two-thirds of sea-borne grain up to the city, and the city magistrates even capped the fees of hired musicians at two drachmas ([Ath. Pol. 50–51](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/ancient-greece/aristotle/athenian-constitution.asp?pg=41)). Excavated official weights and measures support the literary picture of a "highly centralized bureaucracy" controlling trade ([Lang & Crosby](https://ambrosia.ascsa.net/id/agora/publication/agora%2010)). The polis policed quality, measurement and supply, and fixed prices mainly for grain. Grain is the exception that recurs in every era (high confidence).

Coinage emerged in the same world. The earliest coins are small electrum lumps from the Artemision at Ephesus, dated to the third quarter of the seventh century BCE. Scholars dispute whether the key find was a circulating hoard or accumulated offerings, which loosens the date. A gold fraction found beneath walls at Sardis destroyed in 545 BCE supports gold and silver coinage under Croesus ([Macquarie ACANS](https://www.mq.edu.au/faculty-of-arts/schools/school-of-humanities/our-research/australian-centre-for-ancient-numismatic-studies/digital-exhibitions/the-coinage-of-ephesus/a-history-of-ephesus-from-coins-the-greek-state/the-earliest-coins-at-ephesus)). Whether coinage was invented to serve market trade or state payments is genuinely contested. Confidence in the trade explanation is low. Alain Bresson rejects the long-dominant view of Greek traders as marginal outcasts and places merchants inside the polis through the agora and the *emporion* ([Rubio](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2005/2005-02-02.html); [Bresson 2016](https://press.princeton.edu/node/60919)).

Rome layered its market institutions at three scales. Periodic markets ran on the eight-day *nundinae* cycle. The *lex Hortensia* (287 BCE) made market days usable for legal business. Towns and estates petitioned for the right to hold a market (*ius nundinarum*), and local fairs rotated their days so that travelling traders could attend each in turn ([Wikipedia, Nundinae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nundinae)). Permanent food markets (*macella*) spread from the third or second century BCE. Proposed dates for Rome's first range from c. 240 BCE to before 210 BCE. Morgantina has the earliest archaeologically documented example, and about 30 different ground plans are known across the empire ([Richard 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-archaeology/article/macellum-roman-food-markets-in-asia-minor-and-the-levant/940BD98C4C0284A5BE9A4A31DBD6ADA6)). At the monumental scale, Trajan's Markets (built c. 100–110 CE) are routinely called "the world's oldest shopping mall". The site's own museum argues that the shop-type rooms do not imply commerce and presents a multi-functional complex with administrative offices tied to the Forum ([Mercati di Traiano](https://www.mercatiditraiano.it/en/node/6917); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajan%27s_Market)). Treat the mall label as contested. Confidence that the complex was primarily retail is low–moderate.

Roman commercial law was sophisticated. Sale (*emptio venditio*) was a contract made by consent alone, needing only agreement on the thing and the price. The seller guaranteed title. The aediles' edict forced sellers of slaves to disclose disease and defects, and buyers could seek cancellation of the sale or a price reduction ([Long, *Dictionary of Antiquities*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Emptio_et_Venditio.html)). Like Athens, Rome in ordinary times regulated how people traded far more than what they charged (moderate–high).

Asian states sat nearer the "managed market" end of the spectrum. Han Emperor Wu nationalised salt and iron to fund wars against the Xiongnu. A court debate in 81 BCE, recorded in the *Discourses on Salt and Iron*, pitted Confucian critics against Sang Hongyang, a former merchant who defended the monopolies, and the monopolies largely survived ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourses_on_Salt_and_Iron)). Tang Chang'an confined commerce to walled East and West Markets, each with a central block of supervising offices. The markets opened at noon to 300 drum strikes and closed before dusk to 300 gong beats, and informal night markets persisted despite a crackdown in 841 ([Wikiwand, Chang'an](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Chang'an)).

India's *Arthaśāstra* is a normative text whose date and degree of implementation are uncertain. It describes a superintendent of commerce who buys up stock of abundant goods before raising the assessed price and sells state goods through a "single channel". He restrains profits that harm the people, inspects scales and levies duties of one-sixteenth, one-twentieth or one-eleventh ([Arthaśāstra 2.16](https://vedapath.app/en/arthashastra/adhyakshaprachara/16)). Confidence is high as a description of the text and low as a description of practice. In these systems the state was a trader as well as a referee.

Long-distance trade made tolls a source of revenue. The mid-second-century CE Muziris papyrus documents a maritime loan for a voyage to south-west India. It values a cargo of nard, ivory and textiles on the *Hermapollon* at just under 1,155 talents, liable to a 25% customs duty at Alexandria. Lionel Casson notes this was close to the 7,000,000-drachma cost of the aqueduct at Alexandria Troas ([Casson 1990](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/IndOc-Gulf/MuzirisPapyrus-Casson1990.pdf)). The Nabataeans controlled the Incense Route, which carried frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia to Gaza from c. 300 BCE to 200 CE ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negev_incense_route)). Duties like Alexandria's 25% support Bang's reading that rulers harvested long-distance trade for revenue (moderate). Synthesis: classical antiquity produced two models. In the regulated model of Greece and Rome, the state policed conduct, quality and measures. In the managed model of China and India, markets were bounded and timed and the state acted as a trader. Grain was regulated everywhere.

## Medieval rulers sold market rights, and most new markets failed

### England's charter boom was a political market in franchises

England has the best quantitative record of how marketplaces were created. The *Gazetteer of Markets and Fairs in England and Wales to 1516* covers 2,393 places. Of the markets and fairs it records to 1516, **57% of markets and 54% of fairs were created in the thirteenth century**, and **three-quarters of markets and 86% of fairs came from royal grants**, typically "one market and one fair at the same place" ([Letters, Gazetteer](https://archives.history.ac.uk/gazetteer/fullintro.html)). The Institute of Historical Research describes the resulting network as "dense, highly developed and apparently originated earlier than in much of Europe" ([IHR](https://archives.history.ac.uk/ihrcms/cmh/projects/research/markets-and-fairs.html)).

The grants tracked politics as much as commerce. They peaked in the 1220s, when William Marshal's regency charged no fees. They peaked again in the 1250s, driven by Henry III's need for money and support. Further peaks in 1315 and 1318 were largely grants to royal favourites: Bartholomew de Baddlesmere received fourteen markets and eighteen fairs on 12 August 1315. Samantha Letters notes that "the income to be gained from grants of market rights may not have been significant" to the crown ([Letters](https://archives.history.ac.uk/gazetteer/fullintro.html)).

Many grants were speculative and failed. Of places with a market in 1200, 72% were still market towns around 1600. Of places with a market or grant in 1300, only 39% were ([Letters](https://archives.history.ac.uk/gazetteer/fullintro.html)). Of places granted rights in 1300–50, only about 14% were ([UK Data Service guide](https://doc.ukdataservice.ac.uk/doc/4969/mrdoc/pdf/guide.pdf)). Charter counts therefore measure lordly ambition and royal patronage as well as commercial demand (moderate). That complicates any simple reading of the boom as commercial growth. It also provides something the modern marketplace literature lacks: a count of failures.

Medieval law protected each franchise's catchment. Grants were conditional on not harming existing markets, and by the mid-thirteenth century a rule of thumb treated rival markets within six and two-thirds miles as "neighbouring" ([Letters](https://archives.history.ac.uk/gazetteer/fullintro.html)). Jonathan Hill traces the reasoning in Bracton. A reasonable day's journey was 20 miles, divided into thirds: travel to market, trade, and return home in daylight for fear of robbery. The "mile" was probably the 5,000-foot Roman or London mile. Hale later added that a rival market held on the same day was a nuisance in itself ([Hill, *Legal Studies*](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/legal-studies/article/markets-and-the-common-law/35EF471AF8D76D15347ADD043243EEC6)).

In law, a market franchise is an intangible property right, separate from ownership of the land it is held on, and English courts still apply it. In 1984 licensed stalls inside a Hull department store were held to be a market ([Hill](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/legal-studies/article/markets-and-the-common-law/35EF471AF8D76D15347ADD043243EEC6)). Leicester City Council today enforces a 6⅔-mile radius "measured in a straight line not via roads". It defines a market as "a concourse of buyers and sellers" and treats any event with five or more stalls as needing a licence ([Leicester City Council](https://leicester.gov.uk/business/start-up-and-growth/leicester-market/rival-markets-policy)). Few medieval institutions survive so literally (high).

Inside the franchise, public authority policed the deal. Courts of piepowder ("dusty feet") gave summary justice at fairs and markets. Blackstone called them "the lowest, and at the same time the most expeditious, court of justice known to the law of England". The last recorded sitting was at Hemel Hempstead in 1898, and Bristol's court was formally abolished in 1971 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_piepowders)). The thirteenth-century Assize of Bread and Ale fixed the price of ale and varied the weight of the farthing loaf with the price of wheat. Ale-tasters chosen at the manorial court enforced it until its repeal in 1863 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assize_of_Bread_and_Ale)).

Most revealing are three offences that made dealing outside the market a crime. Forestalling meant intercepting goods before they reached market. Regrating meant buying in a market to resell there or within four miles. Engrossing meant buying up provisions to resell. In *R v Rusby* (1800), a dealer bought 90 quarters of oats at 41s and resold 30 the same day at 43s. He was heavily fined, but the appeal court split on whether the offence still existed. The Forestalling, Regrating, etc. Act 1844 abolished the offences and repealed 19 earlier statutes ([Wikipedia, Forestalling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestalling)).

E.P. Thompson's "moral economy" reads eighteenth-century food riots as defences of this protective code. For him the crowd's view was "grounded upon a consistent traditional view of social norms and obligations", set against the "unlimited, unrestrained freedom of the corn trade" that Adam Smith advocated. Critics from John Stevenson to Marc Edelman dispute the concept, and Thompson himself conceded, "Maybe the trouble lies in the word 'moral'" ([Wikipedia, Moral economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_economy)). Confidence is moderate that the concept remains influential but contested.

### The fairs behind "private order" theories were publicly governed

The Champagne Fairs were the hinge of trade between northern and southern Europe from about 1180 until the late thirteenth century. Six fairs a year rotated among Troyes, Provins, Lagny and Bar-sur-Aube, each lasting about six weeks. Together they formed an almost continuous market where Flemish and French cloth met Italian spices and payment services ([Edwards & Ogilvie 2012](https://www.econ.cam.ac.uk/people-files/faculty/sco2/full-texts/Edwards-Ogilvie-2012-Champagne%20Fairs.pdf)).

In 1990 Paul Milgrom, Douglass North and Barry Weingast argued that private judges, by recording merchants' past conduct, kept honest trade going among traders who rarely met again. On their account this "law merchant" worked "before the state took over that role" ([MNW abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ecopol/v2y1990i1p1-23.html)). Jeremy Edwards and Sheilagh Ogilvie's archival reply shows that the counts of Champagne supplied security and treaties guaranteeing merchants safe passage. From the 1170s they appointed fair-wardens, who by the 1220s ran a standing court that registered contracts. They also maintained layered public and church courts and treated foreign and local merchants alike ([Edwards & Ogilvie](https://www.econ.cam.ac.uk/people-files/faculty/sco2/full-texts/Edwards-Ogilvie-2012-Champagne%20Fairs.pdf)).

Edwards and Ogilvie also redate the fairs' decline. Fair tax yields rose from about 5,800 *livres tournois* c. 1275 to 8,383 in 1296, then collapsed to about 1,760 c. 1310. The collapse followed French royal arrests of Flemish and Italian merchants after the crown annexed Champagne in 1285. R.-H. Bautier had dated the decline to c. 1260 ([Edwards & Ogilvie](https://www.econ.cam.ac.uk/people-files/faculty/sco2/full-texts/Edwards-Ogilvie-2012-Champagne%20Fairs.pdf)). Experts disagree on the dating. On the central role of public institutions the evidence is strong: confidence is moderate–high that the private-order account is overstated. Emily Kadens goes further. She argues that a uniform customary *lex mercatoria* adopted across Europe never existed. She names law-and-economics and cyber-law scholars among those who use the story as proof that private ordering works ([Kadens](https://www.private-law-theory.org/2024/01/19/emily-kadens-the-myth-of-the-customary-law-merchant-2/)).

The debate over the eleventh-century "Maghribi traders" of the Cairo Geniza runs the same way. Avner Greif argued that they solved the problem of controlling distant agents through a closed coalition that punished cheats collectively, and linked this to a "collectivist" culture ([Greif 1993](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v83y1993i3p525-48.html)). Edwards and Ogilvie found that "not a single empirical example adduced as evidence of the putative coalition shows that a coalition actually existed", and that the traders used formal courts ([EHS](https://ehs.org.uk/article/contract-enforcement-institutions-and-social-capital-the-maghribi-traders-reappraised1/)). Jessica Goldberg's study of the Geniza letters shows merchants combining gossip with legal mechanisms and adapting to political risk ([Goldberg 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:cbooks:9781107005471)). Greif has replied, so the debate is live. Confidence is moderate–high that the strong coalition claim is not supported as originally made.

Ogilvie's survey of merchant guilds from 1000 to 1800 concludes they lasted because they channelled rents to members and rulers, not because they were efficient ([CUP](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/institutions-and-european-trade/74F4C56B85B115A69123B5F9DEC384C1)). Efficiency theorists still contest this (moderate). The Hanseatic *Kontore* in Novgorod, London, Bruges and Bergen show the mixed public-private arrangement in miniature. They were semi-autonomous merchant compounds with their own treasury, seal and rules, read aloud each year. Security was the main reason for founding them, and they operated under host rulers ([Wikipedia, Kontor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kontor)) (moderate).

Over the long run, commerce moved from periodic fairs to permanent exchanges. Antwerp's merchants received permission for a common bourse in 1485. A larger building opened in 1531 with the motto *in usum negotiatorum cuiuscumque nationis et linguae*, "for the use of merchants of every nation and tongue", serving a city with about 10,000 foreign merchants. Thomas Gresham began a London copy in 1565. Elizabeth I named it the Royal Exchange in 1570 or 1571, depending on the calendar convention used. Exchanges followed at Middelburg (1592), Rotterdam (1595) and Amsterdam (1611) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_Exchange,_Antwerp)).

Fernand Braudel's three-tier model is the most useful frame for this layering. At the base is everyday material life. In the middle is a market economy of markets, fairs, shops and exchanges. At the top is a capitalism of monopoly and network control, whose influence is "vastly disproportionate to its relative size" ([Yee review](https://wsarch.ucr.edu/wsnmail/95/0306.html)). Confidence is moderate; Braudel's sharper distinction between the open market and the "counter-market" was not verified.

### Beyond Europe, inspectors, endowments and ward walls did the same work

Islamic cities placed markets under the *muhtasib*, an appointed inspector who enforced *ḥisba*, "enjoining good and forbidding wrong". His remit covered weights, coinage, prices, fraud, food safety, craft standards and public morals. Mamluk inspectors whipped cheats on a raised stand and paraded offenders through the streets ([Wikipedia, Muhtasib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhtasib)).

The office's origins are contested. Claude Cahen and Mohamed Talbi see the muhtasib as successor to the Byzantine *agoranomos*. Benjamin Foster doubts any direct succession. Willem Floor points to older Iranian and Mesopotamian market overseers. R.P. Buckley finds the first clearly attested muhtasibs in the early Abbasid caliphate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhtasib)). Confidence in any single origin is low–moderate.

Commercial buildings were often endowed as *waqf*, a religious charitable endowment, and caravanserai revenues often funded religious and charitable purposes. Rural caravanserais were spaced about a day's journey apart, roughly 30–40 km in open Iranian country ([Wikipedia, Caravanserai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravanserai)). Istanbul's Grand Bazaar began under Mehmed II in 1455/56. Its core hall, the Cevahir Bedesten, was completed in 1460/61 and endowed to the waqf of the Hagia Sophia mosque. A guild steward appointed by the city's judge set prices and taxes ([Wikipedia, Grand Bazaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Bazaar,_Istanbul)) (moderate). The Umayyad caliphs built structures in Medina's market and levied taxes there "despite Muhammad's earlier prohibition" ([Wikipedia, Bazaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazaar)). This is an early case of a toll-free market being turned into a revenue stream (moderate). The Cairo Geniza, about 400,000 manuscript fragments, underpins S.D. Goitein's six-volume *A Mediterranean Society* (1967–1993), which indexes about 35,000 individuals trading from Samarkand to Seville between 950 and 1250 ([Wikipedia, Cairo Geniza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Geniza)).

China shows the reverse of England's place-bound franchise. Tang Chang'an's 110 walled wards confined commerce to two official markets, but controls weakened after the An Lushan rebellion of 755. Northern Song Kaifeng abolished the night curfew in 965. In the late eleventh century the authorities stopped demolishing shops built out into the streets and charged them rent instead, which in effect legalised street-front commerce. The population rose to about 1.3 million by 1103 ([Hang Lin 2015](https://urbanauapp.org/wp-content/uploads/Lin_2015.pdf)). Confidence is high on the chronology and moderate on the population figure.

Malacca, founded c. 1400, ran a multi-ethnic port through four harbour-masters (*shahbandars*), each managing a group of foreign merchant communities under a maritime code. Tomé Pires wrote that "whoever is lord of Malacca shall have his hands on the throat of Venice". The Portuguese captured the port in 1511 ([Wikipedia, Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca_Sultanate)) (moderate).

In Japan, *rakuichi rakuza* ("free markets, free guilds") edicts stripped guild associations (*za*) of their exclusive sales rights and tax exemptions. The aim was to break merchant monopolies and strengthen the warlords' control. Popular accounts credit Oda Nobunaga at Azuchi, but that is wrong as to origin: the first documented edict was Rokkaku Sadayori's at Ishidera in 1549 ([JapaneseWiki](https://www.japanesewiki.com/history/Rakuichi-rakuza.html)) (moderate).

Trans-Saharan trade in gold for salt linked Sijilmasa, Taghaza and Timbuktu until the Moroccan invasion of 1591. The sources available here are weak, so its specifics are low confidence ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Saharan_trade)).

## Industrial cities enclosed the marketplace, abstracted it, then pushed it out

Between about 1830 and 1890, British towns, chiefly in the Midlands and North, replaced open street markets and shambles with covered market halls. James Schmiechen and Kenneth Carls's gazetteer lists **626 known public retail markets in 392 towns** in England, Wales and Scotland. That figure counts known public markets, not only purpose-built halls ([Tangires review](https://caareviews.org/reviews/307)). The decisive institutional shift was that Parliament moved control of markets from manorial families to local government. Councils embraced the role for social order, sanitation and civic display as much as for commerce ([Tangires](https://caareviews.org/reviews/307)).

The economics were less heroic than the buildings. Ian Mitchell finds that most halls covered their running costs, but the return on capital was often negligible. Most day-to-day necessities came from shops and street traders, not markets. Shopkeepers complained that councils funded by local rates competed unfairly, and Mitchell finds "some justification" for this ([Mitchell 2018](https://ehs.org.uk/article/retail-markets-in-northern-and-midland-england-1870-1914-civic-icon-municipal-white-elephant-or-consumer-paradise)). The reviewer reports a claim that cities with large public market systems saw meat prices up to 60% below shop prices. It could not be checked (low).

Post-war cities then pushed their wholesale markets out. Les Halles, Baltard's iron-and-glass "Belly of Paris", was dismantled in 1971 and the trade moved to Rungis; the Forum des Halles opened on the site in 1979 ([Wikipedia](https://ipfs.ssi.eecc.de/ipns/en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Les_Halles)). Covent Garden had been a private franchise granted by Charles II to the Earl of Bedford in 1670. It was sold in 1962 to a government authority for £3,925,000, the market moved to Nine Elms in 1974, and the old building reopened as a shopping centre in 1980 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covent_Garden)). Billingsgate, made a "free market for fish" by the Billingsgate Act 1698 (royal assent 1699), moved to Poplar in 1982 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billingsgate_Fish_Market)). Synthesis: the same sequence (municipal control, removal to sites lorries could reach, reinvention as heritage and leisure) happened in two capitals within about a decade. That points to shared drivers: road freight, congestion and land values (moderate; the sources consulted do not argue this explicitly).

### Exchanges began as clubs and ended as screens

Organised exchanges began as acts of closure. On 17 May 1792, twenty-four New York brokers agreed to deal only with each other, cut out the auctioneers and charge a minimum commission of 0.25% ([Wikipedia, Buttonwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttonwood_Agreement)). The London Stock Exchange began operating as a members-only subscription body on 3 March 1801 ([Skinner, *The Finanser*](https://thefinanser.com/2011/12/how-the-city-developed-part-eight-the-london-stock-exchange)). The Chicago Board of Trade was founded on 3 April 1848 to formalise forward contracts and listed standardised futures in 1864 ([Wikipedia, CBOT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Board_of_Trade)). William Cronon's *Nature's Metropolis* shows grading and grain elevators turning farmers' grain into a standard, "liquid" commodity. Receipts for it could trade without anyone seeing the goods ([Blevins notes on Cronon](https://www.cameronblevins.org/quals/Cronon_NaturesMetropolis.html)): the market had separated the commodity from the place. Antitrust law reached the exchanges in *Chicago Board of Trade v. United States* (1917), which applied the Sherman Act under a "rule of reason" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Board_of_Trade)).

The closure was undone 150 to 190 years later. The SEC ended fixed commissions in 1975. London's Big Bang on 27 October 1986 grew out of a 1983 settlement of an Office of Fair Trading case under the Restrictive Trade Practices Act 1956. It abolished fixed minimum commissions and the separation of brokers and jobbers, and it moved trading from open outcry on the floor to screens ([Wikipedia, Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang_%28financial_markets%29)). NASDAQ, which began on 8 February 1971, is often called the first fully electronic stock market. In fact it began as a quotation system: trades were done by telephone until electronic execution arrived in 1984 ([Wikipedia, Nasdaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasdaq)). Chicago's agricultural trading pits survived until 2015 ([Wikipedia, CBOT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Board_of_Trade)).

### Retail took the market's functions one at a time

Paris shops of the 1840s introduced fixed prices, price tags and newspaper advertising. Le Bon Marché grew from 12 employees in 1838 to 1,788 in 1879 ([Wikipedia, Department store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_store)). Fixed prices moved price-setting from the haggle between buyer and seller to the firm (moderate; synthesis). Mail order reached dispersed rural buyers: Montgomery Ward, founded in 1872, was mailing three million catalogues by 1904 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_Ward)).

Self-service supermarkets took over the market hall's core role of selling cheap food in volume. Britain had just 10 self-service shops in 1947 but more than 3,400 supermarkets by 1969 ([Wikipedia, Supermarket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermarket)). In 1956 the US Department of Agriculture's "American Way" exhibit in Rome included the first fully stocked supermarket outside the US. Milan's first supermarket opened in 1957 with $300,000 from Nelson Rockefeller's IBEC, against opposition from shopkeepers and Communists ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermarket); [Caprotti](https://www.giuseppecaprotti.it/?p=112082)). The Milan account comes from a family member of the store's later owner, so treat it as moderate. These episodes support Victoria de Grazia's account of an American "market empire" displacing Europe's class-segmented "bourgeois regime of consumption". Critics argue she plays down what survived in Europe and how American formats were adapted locally ([Luccarelli](https://logosjournal.com/?p=664)).

Malls took over sociability. US shopping centres rose from 4,500 in 1960 to about 70,000 in 1986. Victor Gruen, whose Southdale centre (1956) helped popularise the enclosed mall, later deplored its "land wasting seas of parking" ([Wikipedia, Shopping mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_mall)). Brent Cross, Britain's first out-of-town enclosed shopping centre on the American model, opened on 2 March 1976 with about 8,000 parking spaces ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brent_Cross_Shopping_Centre)).

### Pre-internet networks rehearsed the platform disputes

Exchange & Mart, launched in May 1868 from a converted potato warehouse in Covent Garden, matched dispersed buyers and sellers through paid listings and peaked at over 350,000 circulation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_%26_Mart)). American Airlines' SABRE reservation system became fully operational in 1964 and opened to travel agents in 1976. It favoured its owner's flights. Congress investigated, and in 1984 the US government outlawed biased displays ([Wikipedia, Sabre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabre_%28travel_reservation_system%29)). That was in substance the first regulation of what is now called self-preferencing.

France's state-built Minitel went national in 1982 and lent terminals free from 1984. It reached about 9 million terminals and nearly 26,000 services, including mail order, ticketing and dating. Charges appeared on the phone bill, and in 1988 about two-thirds of the fee went to service providers ([Wikipedia, Minitel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minitel)). That was an app-store-style revenue split two decades before app stores.

### Physical markets survived by changing jobs

The physical market did not die; it took on new roles. Car boot sales spread from the early 1980s. The first large commercial "Boot Fair" is documented at Wrotham Heath, Kent, on 20 September 1980; an earlier origin story set in the 1970s is unverified ([Wikipedia, Car boot sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_boot_sale)). US farmers' markets grew from 1,755 in 1994 to 8,144 in 2013 ([Wikipedia, Farmers' market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmers%27_market)). In the Global South the "traditional" market was never a survival; it remained the main form of retail. Scholars reconceived it through Keith Hart's "informal income opportunities" (presented in 1971, published 1973) and the ILO's 1972 Kenya report, then through Hernando de Soto's argument that over-regulation pushed Peruvians into informality ([Wikipedia, Informal economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informal_economy)).

The Greater London Authority counted about 280 retail markets in 2017, against 163 mapped in 2010; part of the rise may reflect better counting. The markets supported about 13,250 jobs. The GLA justified them largely on social grounds, and the Mayor called markets "the original business incubators" ([GLA 2017](https://london.gov.uk/sites/default/files/gla_markets_report_short_web.pdf)). Synthesis: the market's value shifted from cheapness, which supermarkets took, to sociability, provenance and experience (moderate).

Policy shifted too. It moved from protecting the marketplace itself (the forestalling laws, abolished 1844) to regulating competition between firms: the Sherman Act, the Robinson-Patman Act of 1936 and the restrictive-practices case behind Big Bang ([Wikipedia, Supermarket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermarket)). It also came to cover opening hours, as in the Sunday Trading Act 1994, which limits shops larger than 280 m² to six Sunday hours ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_Trading_Act_1994)).

## Anthropologists and sociologists found relationships inside every market

Economic anthropology supplied the distinction this report relies on most. Paul Bohannan and George Dalton's *Markets in Africa* (1962) separated two things. The **marketplace** is the specific site where buyers and sellers meet. The **market principle** is "price formation by impersonal forces of supply and demand", and it can operate without a marketplace. They distinguished three kinds of society: those without marketplaces; those with "peripheral markets", where land and labour were not exchanged on market terms; and those dominated by the market principle ([Muratake review](https://www.ide.go.jp/library/English/Publish/Periodicals/De/pdf/64_04_10.pdf)). The distinction dissolves much of the ancient-economy argument. Mesopotamia had the market principle without a marketplace, while many peasant societies had marketplaces in which the market principle did not govern land and labour (high on the concept; moderate on this application). Behind it lies Polanyi's split between economics as rational choice under scarcity and economics as the way a society provides for itself. That split defined the formalist–substantivist debate of the 1960s, which was later synthesised rather than resolved ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formalist%E2%80%93substantivist_debate)).

Ethnography repeatedly found commercial and non-commercial exchange side by side. Malinowski's Trobriand islanders conducted ceremonial *kula* gift exchange and hard-bargaining *gimwali* barter on the same voyages ([Wikipedia, Kula ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kula_ring)). The Bohannans' famous account of Tiv "spheres of exchange" destroyed by colonial money is now contested. Jane Guyer reads the Tiv as pursuing long-term profit strategies, and David Graeber links the spheres to West African slavery ([Wikipedia, Spheres of exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spheres_of_exchange)). The original account should be presented as influential but historically disputed (moderate–high).

Clifford Geertz studied the bazaar of Sefrou in Morocco, where about two-thirds of the town's labour force worked. He found information "poor, scarce, maldistributed, inefficiently communicated, and intensely valued". Buyers and sellers formed lasting "clientelized" ties, and rather than shopping around they probed a single offer in depth ([Geertz 1978](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/mechanism-design/1978-geertz.pdf)).

G. William Skinner applied Christaller and Lösch's central place theory to rural China. He described a nested hierarchy of standard, intermediate and central market towns with hexagonal service areas, and periodic market schedules that interlocked within the ten-day *xun*. He argued that these marketing systems tied peasant communities into the wider society, with local elites acting as brokers between peasants and officials ([Skinner 1964](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/marketing-and-social-structure-in-rural-china-part-i/20071250B7BC9B70B1C47DF11B608FCA)). William Cronon, by contrast, criticises central place theory as ahistorical ([Blevins](https://www.cameronblevins.org/quals/Cronon_NaturesMetropolis.html)).

Markets are also gendered and politically exposed. In Kumasi Central Market, Gracia Clark found each commodity group led by an elected "queen", whose core service was settling disputes, while peer pressure supported credit among members. Census figures show nearly 80% of adult women in southern Ghanaian towns working as traders. On 5 September 1979 Ghana's military government dynamited Accra's Makola No. 1 market. Price-control raids recast respected "mother traders" as "big cheats and nation wreckers" ([Clark 2010](https://asq.africa.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/168/Clark-Vol11Is2-3.pdf)). Sidney Mintz found that across many peasant market systems "much or even most of the distributive activity is carried on by women" ([Mintz 1971](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/men-women-and-trade/CC43D68DE4FAFBA99AC0F6F870F3C882)). In Britain, Sophie Watson's study of eight markets found them to be sites of encounter and "rubbing along" across difference. She attributed market failure to lack of investment, management and strategy rather than inevitable decline. Her work informed a 2009 House of Commons select committee inquiry into traditional retail markets and the "Love Your Local Market" campaign ([REF case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=32085)).

Economic sociology generalised these findings. Mark Granovetter's 1985 argument that economic action is embedded in social relations launched the field. By 2002 he conceded that "embeddedness" had become "almost meaningless", and he offered a sharper formulation: "markets are not more or less social. They may be more or less personal" ([Polanyi Symposium 2004](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/11991/1/SOR_Polyani_2004.pdf)). Fred Block reads Polanyi as having discovered an "always-embedded market economy", in which "trust is the lubricant for all market transactions". Greta Krippner criticises the field for taking "the market" for granted ([Polanyi Symposium](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/11991/1/SOR_Polyani_2004.pdf)). Neil Fligstein treats building a market as "a social and political project", structured by incumbents and challengers. Viviana Zelizer shows that US life insurance took off only once its marketers overcame moral objections ([Fligstein & Dauter 2007](https://sociology.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/faculty/fligstein/ANRV316-SO33-06_001-024_.pdf)).

Donald MacKenzie's history of Black–Scholes–Merton option pricing shows a model shaping the market it described, then undermining itself. Market prices fitted the model closely from 1976 to 1987 as traders used it, and fitted poorly after the 1987 crash ([De Cock](https://core.ac.uk/download/4188759.pdf)). Marion Fourcade and Kieran Healy recast the normative debate as four views of markets: civilising, destructive, feeble or "moralized". In *The Ordinal Society* (2024) they extend the argument to platforms whose ratings and scores sort people before they ride with a driver or rent a home, repackaging "familiar structures of social advantage" as merit ([Healy](https://kieranhealy.org/publications/moral-views/); [Allstora](https://allstora.com/products/the-ordinal-society)).

Outside mainstream economics there is broad consensus that real markets are embedded in social, political and moral relations. "Embedded versus disembedded" is now seen as a poorly posed question. The live disputes concern mechanism (networks, law and the state, or calculative tools) and effects (efficiency or protection of incumbents) (high).

## Moralists, artists and judges turned a place into an abstraction

### The vocabulary moved from meeting to mechanism

English "market" first meant, in the early twelfth century, "a meeting at a fixed time for trading livestock and provisions". By the late thirteenth century it could mean a building or space, by the 1610s a region, and by the 1680s "sale as governed by supply and demand". "Market economy" is first attested in 1948 ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/market)). Confidence in these dates is high, but they are first attestations and can be pushed earlier by new finds. "Fair" comes from Latin *feriae*, religious holidays, because trade gatherings were held on feast days ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/fair)). "Emporium" comes from Greek *emporos*, originally a traveller ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/emporium)). "Forum" is "apparently related" to *foris*, outside ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/forum)). "Souk" traces through Aramaic to Akkadian *sūqu*, street ([Wikipedia, Bazaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazaar)).

Jean-Christophe Agnew's *Worlds Apart* (1986) describes the market moving from a place on the margins of society, presided over by Hermes as god of thresholds, to a placeless process ([Deng review](https://emc.english.ucsb.edu/miscellanyan-early-modern-blog/book-reviews/book-review-worlds-apart-1986/)). Confidence is moderate, because this formulation comes through a review. Synthesis (moderate): the vocabulary keeps placing the market at an edge: outside the walls, in transit, on holy days. The modern abstract market reads as that threshold widened until it has no edge.

### Moral traditions bounded the market rather than abolishing it

The moral traditions surveyed here generally drew boundaries around trade rather than condemning it outright. Jesus drove the money-changers from the Temple: "make not my Father's house an house of merchandise" ([John 2:16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2011%3A15-17%2CJohn%202%3A13-16&version=KJV)). Aristotle called exchange for gain "justly censured; for it is unnatural", and judged usury, "the birth of money from money", "the most unnatural" way of getting wealth ([*Politics* I](https://classicalwisdom.com/greek_books/politics-by-aristotle-book-i/4/)). In 1958 Raymond de Roover argued against the view that the scholastic "just price" was a vague notion devised by monks ignorant of markets. He cited the glossator Accursius, who held that goods are worth what they can commonly be sold for ([de Roover](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/concept-of-the-just-price-theory-and-economic-policy/12B1813EB9C22BCD6448A4970DB005A4)). Confidence is moderate, and the interpretation is still debated.

In a hadith graded sound, the Prophet refused a request to fix prices: "Allah is the one Who fixes prices" ([Abu Dawud 3451](https://hadithunlocked.com/abudawud:3451)). This is sometimes cited as proto-liberal. That is a selective reading of a tradition that also prohibits interest and specified goods and maintains a market inspector ([Wikipedia, Hisba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisba)). The Buddhist *Vaṇijjā Sutta* bars lay followers from trading in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants and poison ([AN 5.177](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN5_177.html)). Max Weber reversed the direction, making Calvinism a generator of market rationality. The thesis lacks robust empirical support. Becker and Woessmann attribute the Protestant economic advantage to literacy, and Cantoni finds no effect on German city growth ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Protestant_Ethic_and_the_Spirit_of_Capitalism)) (moderate–high).

### Theorists made the abstraction explicit, and were misquoted

Adam Smith used "invisible hand" only once in *The Wealth of Nations*, about a merchant who prefers domestic to foreign investment "for security". Marshall and Jevons never used the phrase. Paul Samuelson's 1948 textbook popularised it in a general-equilibrium sense, while warning that its "unguarded conclusion" had done "almost as much harm as good" ([Wikipedia, Invisible hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invisible_hand)). F.A. Hayek's 1945 essay defined the price system as "a mechanism for communicating information": "The whole acts as one market, not because any of its members survey the whole field" ([Hayek 1945](https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html)). That is the purest statement of the market as a placeless process.

Karl Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* (1944) called the self-regulating market a "stark utopia" and insisted that "laissez-faire was planned; planning was not" ([Immerwahr](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/daniel-immerwahr/PolanyiUS.pdf)). Douglass North and Deirdre McCloskey counter that every society uses markets alongside reciprocity and redistribution, and that markets predate Polanyi's chronology ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Transformation_%28book%29)). Albert Hirschman recovered the eighteenth-century case that commerce softens manners, the *doux commerce* thesis, in Montesquieu's words "wherever there is commerce, there the ways of men are gentle". Later critics note that this "gentle" commerce rested partly on the slave trade and colonial exploitation ([Wikipedia, Doux commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doux_commerce)).

Michael Sandel's claim that the US "drifted from having a market economy to being a market society" revives the old question of where markets should stop. Rob Reich judges the book "neither original nor deep" because it offers no test for when market norms crowd out others ([Reich 2012](https://ssir.org/books/reviews/entry/a_market_rather_than_civil_society)). Debra Satz's *Why Some Things Should Not Be for Sale* (2010) takes up the same question ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/1465/chapter/140857674)).

### Fairs and fiction held the market in two registers

Literature and art portrayed the marketplace in two registers. The first is festive. In Bakhtin's reading of Rabelais, the marketplace was a reservoir of "frank and free" speech and gesture, carnivalesque and levelling ([Bakhtin](https://Monoskop.org/images/7/70/Bakhtin_Mikhail_Rabelais_and_His_World_1984.pdf)). Ben Jonson's *Bartholomew Fair* (1614) ends with the Puritan Zeal-of-the-Land Busy converted to playgoing and Justice Overdo in the stocks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomew_Fair_%28play%29)). The second register is seduction. Christina Rossetti's "Goblin Market" (published 1862) has been read as a critique of Victorian marketing and of the marriage market, and also in feminist, religious and addiction terms ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goblin_Market)). In Zola's *Au Bonheur des Dames* (1883), a department store modelled on Le Bon Marché ruins neighbourhood specialists through choice, advertising and refunds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ladies%27_Paradise)).

Across these works the market moves indoors. It runs from Beuckelaer's Antwerp fish stalls of 1569 ([National Gallery](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/joachim-beuckelaer-the-four-elements-water)) to Zola's Les Halles in *Le Ventre de Paris* (1873) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Ventre_de_Paris)) and Walter Benjamin's Paris arcades ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/benjamin/)). Georg Simmel diagnosed the urban result in 1903: "Money and exchange thus become the media with which city dwellers invest their trust" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Metropolis_and_Mental_Life)).

The great fairs followed a recognisable life cycle: religious charter, commercial peak, drift into entertainment, then suppression or obsolescence, and finally heritage revival (moderate; synthesis). Bartholomew Fair was, by tradition, chartered in 1133 to fund a priory. It was England's chief cloth sale, and the Merchant Taylors tested cloth measures there against their standard silver yard until 1854; the City suppressed it in 1855 as a "school of vice" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomew_Fair)). Stourbridge Fair was licensed by King John in 1199 to support a leper chapel. Defoe called it in 1724 "not only the greatest in the whole nation, but in the world". It declined as canals and roads reduced the importance of river transport, and it ended in 1933 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stourbridge_Fair)).

The disorderly popular fair was suppressed in the same years that the curated exhibition was invented. The Great Exhibition of 1851 drew about six million visitors to some 13,000 exhibits, and its £186,000 surplus funded South Kensington's museums ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Exhibition)). Henri Pirenne argued that merchant "new men" recreated medieval towns around trade. Archaeological evidence that settlements continued without a break has qualified this ([Wikipedia, Pirenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Pirenne)). The safer claim is that markets helped make towns central places rather than creating them (moderate).

### The "marketplace of ideas" is not Holmes's phrase

The metaphor travelled furthest in American law. Holmes's 1919 dissent in *Abrams v. United States* argued that "the ultimate good desired is better reached by free trade in ideas — that the best test of truth is the power of the thought to get itself accepted in the competition of the market" ([Abrams dissent](https://home.ubalt.edu/id86mp66/Class%20Reference/Incitement/Abrams_dissent.htm)). He never wrote "marketplace of ideas". The phrase first appears in a Supreme Court opinion in Justice Douglas's 1953 concurrence in *United States v. Rumely*, where a publisher "bids for the minds of men in the market place of ideas". Milton and Mill, who are often credited, made the argument without the economic analogy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketplace_of_ideas)) (high). By the 1990s critics were describing "the Market" as a god, as in Harvey Cox's "The Market as God" (1999) ([Marty](https://divinity.uchicago.edu/sightings/articles/testing-faith-market-god-martin-e-marty)) (moderate; second-hand). Thomas Frank attacked "market populism", the idea that markets are more democratic than elected governments ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Market_Under_God)). The word that once meant a fixed-day gathering for livestock had become a quasi-divine agent.

## Digital platforms rebuilt the chartered market in private hands

### The 1987 prediction was half right

In 1987 Thomas Malone, JoAnne Yates and Robert Benjamin predicted that falling coordination costs would shift economic activity from firms to electronic markets ([citedrive](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/electronic-markets-and-electronic-hierarchies)). Twenty years later their paper, the most-cited in information systems, was the subject of a panel convened to debate whether the evidence fitted ([ICIS 2007](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/electronic-markets-theory-and-evidence-from-20-years-of-research/)). The business-to-business exchanges closest to the forecast mostly failed. Covisint was launched by Ford, GM and DaimlerChrysler in 2000. Amid deadlock between its rival owners, it signed only 40 of roughly 30,000 parts suppliers in its first five months ([Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/economics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/covisint)).

What succeeded was the privately governed marketplace run by a third party. Pierre Omidyar's letter of 26 February 1996 founding eBay's Feedback Forum began "Most people are honest" ([eBay](https://pages.ebay.com/services/forum/feedback-foundersnote.html)). David Baron described the resulting rules as "a private ordering of eBay's community" operating "in the shadow of the public order" ([Baron 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/ED76F2332286DF2FD83FA68F22A6D05A)).

Amazon tried auctions (March 1999) and zShops (September 1999) before Marketplace (November 2000) put third-party offers on its own product pages ([Amazon 2001](https://press.aboutamazon.com/2001/3/amazon-marketplace-a-winner-for-customers-sellers-and-industry-new-service-grows-over-200-percent-in-first-four-months)). Third-party sellers' share of Amazon's physical gross merchandise sales rose from 3% in 1999 to 58% in 2018; in Bezos's words, "Third-party sellers are kicking our first party butt. Badly." ([Bezos 2018 letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2018-letter-to-shareholders)). On a different measure, paid units, the third-party share peaked at 62% in Q4 2024 and fell to 60% in Q1 2026 as Amazon's own retail grew faster ([Marketplace Pulse](https://www.marketplacepulse.com/articles/amazon-is-regaining-1p-unit-share)). The operator moves back and forth between running the venue and selling in it. Amazon's share of all US third-party marketplace sales rose from 42% in 2014 to a peak of 74% in 2022 ([Euromonitor](https://www.euromonitor.com/article/temu-shein-and-tiktok-shops-emerging-power-in-us-e-commerce)) (moderate; single analyst).

In China, Taobao (2003) beat eBay, which had entered by buying EachNet and charged sellers fees. Taobao offered free listings and Alipay escrow, which a Harvard case write-up credits with resolving "the trust and payment problem" ([Harvard D3](https://d3.harvard.edu/platform-digit/submission/digital-winner-in-chinas-online-c2c-marketplace-taobao-com/)) (moderate). The Chinese cross-border challengers of 2022–23 (Temu, Shein's marketplace and TikTok Shop) run "fully/semi-managed" models. A "price evaluation system" gives the platform more control over prices than Amazon has ([Euromonitor](https://www.euromonitor.com/article/temu-shein-and-tiktok-shops-emerging-power-in-us-e-commerce)). TikTok Shop's global gross merchandise value reportedly reached $64.3bn in 2025, up 94% ([Jing Daily, citing Momentum Works](https://jingdaily.com/intels/2026-04/29/tiktok-tops-2-billion-users-shop-gmv-climbs-94-in-2025)) (moderate; single originating analyst). Synthesis (moderate): the electronic markets hypothesis got the direction of cost change right and the winning institutional form wrong.

### Theory explained why price structure, thickness and trust matter

Platform economics rests on a small number of established results (high). Michael Katz and Carl Shapiro formalised network effects in 1985 ([Wikipedia, Two-sided market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_market)). Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole (2003) showed that in two-sided markets, transaction volume depends on how prices are split between the two sides, not just on their total. A platform can therefore rationally price below cost to one side ([Rochet & Tirole](https://doi.org/10.1162/154247603322493212)). Bernard Caillaud and Bruno Jullien described the "divide-and-conquer" pricing that solves chicken-and-egg problems: subsidise one side to attract it, then charge the other. Mark Armstrong described the "competitive bottleneck", in which platforms extract surplus from whichever side uses several platforms at once ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_market)).

Tirole's 2014 Nobel is often described as a prize for platform economics; commentators at the time called him a "founding father of platform economics" ([Applico](https://applicoinc.com/?p=8092)). The citation actually reads "for his analysis of market power and regulation", and the press release does not mention two-sided markets ([Nobel 2014](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2014/press-release/)). Alvin Roth and Lloyd Shapley's 2012 prize recognised "the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design" ([Nobel 2012](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2012/press-release/)). Roth's design criteria are that markets must be thick, uncongested and safe to take part in. Together with his work on "repugnance" as a constraint on markets, they translate directly to platforms ([Roth 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0297.2007.02121.x); [Roth 2007](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.21.3.37)). Confidence is moderate–high, because these texts were not checked directly for this report. George Akerlof's "lemons" problem explains why marketplaces invest in reputation, escrow and guarantees: when buyers cannot judge quality, good sellers leave ([Akerlof](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1879431)).

The empirical record is sobering. Paul Resnick and Richard Zeckhauser studied 1999 eBay data. They found feedback was given more than half the time and was "almost always positive". Better-rated sellers were more likely to sell but earned no price premium on the items studied, and buyer and seller ratings were strongly correlated through reciprocity ([Resnick & Zeckhauser](https://presnick.people.si.umich.edu/papers/ebayNBER/index.html)). Liran Einav, Chiara Farronato and Jonathan Levin summarise the platform's job as search, matching, pricing and trust ([Einav et al.](https://nber.org/papers/w21496)). Theory remains contested on antitrust. Regulating one side's price can shift charges to the other (the "waterbed" effect), and the US Supreme Court's *Ohio v. American Express* decision addressed how antitrust applies to two-sided markets ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_market)).

### Practitioners wrote a survivors' canon

The practitioner literature, mostly from US venture capital, converged on a sequence. Bill Gurley's 2012 checklist prized fragmentation on both sides and control of payment flow ("if you bring the cash, you are king") ([Gurley 2012](https://abovethecrowd.com/2012/11/13/all-markets-are-not-created-equal-10-factors-to-consider-when-evaluating-digital-marketplaces/)). His 2013 essay "A Rake Too Far" warned that "the most dangerous strategy for any platform company is to price too high". He estimated eBay's take at just under 10% in 2011 and argued that Apple's 30% App Store fee "basically launched a nasty competitor" in Amazon's subsidised Kindle. His remedy, a low base fee plus optional paid visibility, is in effect marketplace advertising ([Gurley 2013](https://abovethecrowd.com/2013/04/18/a-rake-too-far-optimal-platformpricing-strategy/)).

Lenny Rachitsky interviewed people from 17 marketplaces. Nearly all had constrained their launch by geography or category, and 14 put almost all early resources into supply ([Rachitsky Part 1](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-kickstart-and-scale-a-marketplace); [Part 2](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/i/159750/step-decide-which-side-of-the-marketplace-to-concentrate-on)). Andrew Chen's "atomic network" (the smallest network that works on its own) and "hard side" (the harder-to-attract participants) formalise the same playbook ([Charter](https://www.charterworks.com/the-cold-start-problem-andrew-chen/)). Ben Thompson's Aggregation Theory (2015) argues that internet aggregators win by owning the user relationship and commoditising suppliers ([Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2015/aggregation-theory/)).

These are rules of thumb drawn from survivors, not tested theory. NFX's widely quoted claim that network effects produced "70% of all the value created in technology since 1994" comes with no data ([NFX](https://www.nfx.com/post/network-effects-bible)) (low). a16z's 2023 Marketplace 100 describes a "third wave" of curated, opinionated marketplaces replacing "anyone can list" models. It also cites Amazon seller fees rising from about 35% to 52% of product price over six years, a secondary figure given without a primary source ([a16z](https://a16z.com/marketplace100/)) (moderate).

### Critics recast operators as factory owners, lords and tax farmers

From 2016 a critical literature recast marketplaces as private powers. Martin Kenney and John Zysman argued that platform owners are "developing power that may be even more formidable than was that of the factory owners". For a historical analogy they reached for "the putting-out economy that existed before factories", not feudalism ([Kenney & Zysman 2016](https://issues.org/rise-platform-economy-big-data-work/)). Lina Khan's "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" (2017) argued that Amazon's dual role as marketplace and retailer "creates conflicts of interest, given that Amazon is positioned to favor its own products". She proposed restoring structural competition principles or imposing "common carrier obligations and duties" ([Khan 2017](https://www.yalelawjournal.org/note/amazons-antitrust-paradox)).

Cory Doctorow's "enshittification" describes a three-stage decline: platforms are first good to users, then squeeze users for the benefit of business customers, then squeeze business customers too ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/105733-rise-enshittification-officially-word-year.html)). Synthesis (moderate): this is in effect a dynamic, moralised restatement of two-sided pricing theory, with launch subsidies followed by extraction once users are locked in.

Yanis Varoufakis's *Technofeudalism* casts platforms as "digital 'fiefdoms'" collecting "cloud rent" in place of profit. Reviewers find the thesis "too deterministic or a stretch" ([tripleC](https://www.triple-c.at/index.php/tripleC/article/view/1647/1643)). Evgeny Morozov argues that Big Tech behaves like ordinary competitive capitalist firms. Cédric Durand replies that its extraction is a "digital tribute" rooted in users' and businesses' dependence on it ([Verso](https://www.versobooks.com/blogs/news/where-is-digital-technology-taking-us-cedric-durand-responds-to-evgeny-morozov)). Confidence that platform power is real and poorly captured by analysis focused on consumer prices is moderate–high. Confidence in the strong claim that capitalism has given way to feudalism is low.

### Regulators are deputising platforms as market inspectors

Between 2021 and 2026, regulators stopped treating marketplaces as passive hosts.

**EU competition rules.** In December 2022 the EU accepted commitments from Amazon not to use non-public seller data for its own retail business and to rank all sellers equally for the Buy Box, without a finding of infringement ([European Commission](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_22_7777)). The Digital Markets Act designated Amazon's marketplace a gatekeeper service on 6 September 2023 ([DMA gatekeepers](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/gatekeepers_en)). Its first fines, on 23 April 2025, hit Apple (€500m, for App Store anti-steering) and Meta (€200m) ([Bird & Bird](https://competitionlawinsights.twobirds.com/post/102kajb/first-ever-dma-non-compliance-fines-another-step-in-big-tech-scrutiny)). On 23 July 2026 Google was fined €890m: €460m for favouring its own shopping, hotel and other results in search, and €430m for anti-steering in Google Play ([European Commission](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/commission-fines-google-eur890-million-breaches-digital-markets-act-2026-07-23_en)).

**EU product rules.** The Digital Services Act requires marketplaces to identify traders before they list and to design their interfaces so traders can comply ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/dsa-decoded-9-the-dsa-and-online-marketplaces-102lx12)). Its first major marketplace fine, €200m against Temu on 28 May 2026, targeted a risk assessment that underestimated illegal products. Mystery shopping found that a "very high share" of tested chargers failed basic safety tests ([European Commission](https://europa.eu/newsroom/ecpc-failover/pdf/ip-26-1178_en.pdf)). The General Product Safety Regulation requires marketplaces to act on authority orders within two working days ([Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/navigating-eus-new-gpsr-obligations-are-introduced-online-marketplaces)).

**United States.** The INFORM Consumers Act, in force from 27 June 2023, obliges marketplaces to verify high-volume sellers ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/informing-businesses-about-inform-consumers-act)). Beyond that, US regulation runs through litigation. The FTC's case over Prime sign-up "dark patterns" settled for $2.5bn in September 2025 ([Claims Journal](https://amp.claimsjournal.com/news/national/2025/09/25/333169.htm)). Its monopolisation case against Amazon is set for trial on 29 March 2027 ([MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/antitrust/articles/2536525)). The Supreme Court agreed on 30 June 2026 to hear Apple's challenge to the contempt ruling in its anti-steering dispute with Epic ([IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/06/30/high-court-grants-cert-in-apples-challenge-to-ninth-circuit-contempt-ruling-in-app-store-dispute/)).

**UK, China and India.** Britain designated Apple and Google with "strategic market status" in mobile platforms in October 2025. In June 2026 it imposed fair-ranking requirements on Google search, but by the latest update it had imposed no binding conduct requirement in mobile ([CMA mobile](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/the-cmas-programme-of-work-across-mobile-platforms); [CMA search](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/further-cma-action-to-secure-a-fairer-deal-for-businesses-and-improve-google-search-services-in-uk)). China fined Alibaba CNY 18.228bn in 2021 for forcing merchants to "choose one from two" platforms ([China Justice Observer](https://chinajusticeobserver.com/a/alibaba-fined-cny-18-2-billion-for-anti-monopoly-violations)). India bars foreign-funded marketplaces from holding inventory for domestic sale, a rule relaxed in July 2026 only for exports ([Bar & Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/law-firms/view-point/dpiit-relaxes-fdi-norms-for-export-oriented-inventory-based-e-commerce)).

**Tax and customs.** Since July 2021, EU marketplaces have been the "deemed supplier" for VAT on low-value imports and on non-EU sellers' sales, which makes them responsible for collecting it ([VATCalc](https://www.vatcalc.com/eu/eu-e-commerce-vat-package-1-july-2021/)). The US suspended its $800 de minimis customs exemption globally from 29 August 2025 ([White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/united-states-suspend-customs-de-minimis-entry-most-shipments-august-29-2025)). The EU imposed a €3 duty per item category on low-value parcels from 1 July 2026 ([Retail Gazette](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2026/07/shein-and-temu-hit-by-new-eu-customs-duty-on-cheap-parcels/)). These changes strike hardest at the direct-from-factory model of Temu and Shein (moderate).

The newest contest is over the buyer's AI agent. OpenAI launched checkout inside ChatGPT in September 2025 and handed checkout back to merchants in March 2026 ([sanbi.ai tracker](https://sanbi.ai/blog/agentic-commerce-tracker-ai-checkout)) (moderate; single tracker). Amazon sued Perplexity over its shopping agent. In August 2026 the Ninth Circuit vacated a preliminary injunction against Perplexity. It reasoned, on the current record, that users rather than Perplexity access Amazon's site, with the agent acting as the user's tool ([Retail Insight Network](https://www.retail-insight-network.com/news/us-court-overturns-amazon-injunction-perplexity/)) (high on the ruling). Adoption remains small: one survey found 8% of AI users had bought something entirely through an agent ([sanbi.ai](https://sanbi.ai/blog/agentic-commerce-tracker-ai-checkout)) (low–moderate). Whether agents will cut marketplaces out is too early to call.

## Six regularities recur from Kanesh to Amazon

Read across eras, the evidence supports six regularities. They are my synthesis; the confidence levels attach to the patterns, not just to the facts beneath them.

### Someone always grants the right to trade and takes a toll

Every marketplace in the record sits inside a franchise granted or held by some authority. At Kanesh it was Assur's treaties and levies; in Rome, the *ius nundinarum*; in Athens, boards of officials chosen by lot. In England, royal charters created three-quarters of the markets recorded to 1516. The counts of Champagne protected their fairs, and waqf endowments stood behind Ottoman bazaar halls. Later came the Earl of Bedford's Covent Garden charter, the Buttonwood brokers' club and Victorian municipal corporations. Today platform operators constitute themselves and are later designated as gatekeepers by regulators. Confidence in the pattern is moderate–high.

The toll follows the franchise, and its level has not obviously fallen. The comparisons below are not like-for-like, since a customs duty on a cargo is not a fee for services. But they show that taking a fifth to a third of high-value flows is not a digital novelty (low confidence as a quantitative comparison).

| *Arthaśāstra* duties by volume, weight or count | 1/16, 1/20, 1/11 (≈5–9%) | [Arthaśāstra 2.16](https://vedapath.app/en/arthashastra/adhyakshaprachara/16) |

Two historical findings matter for anyone setting a toll. First, the Champagne fairs' revenue collapsed after the French crown turned predatory and arrested Flemish and Italian merchants. Second, rulers repeatedly converted free trade into revenue once it became valuable, from the Umayyads taxing Medina's market to Kaifeng's officials renting out encroaching shops. Gurley's "rake too far" and Doctorow's enshittification describe the same temptation from the operator's side (moderate).

### Liquidity was engineered in time and space long before "cold start"

Pre-modern authorities solved thin demand by concentrating trade in time and protecting its catchment area. Examples include Rome's rotating rural fairs, Skinner's interlocking *xun* schedules, the Champagne rotation that turned four towns into a near-continuous market, Bracton's day's-journey radius and caravanserais spaced a day apart. The platform canon's advice — constrain the launch, build an "atomic network", over-invest in the hard side — tackles the same problem with software: achieving what Roth calls thickness (moderate; synthesis). The difference lies in enforcement. The medieval catchment was a legal right defended in court. The digital catchment rests on network effects and contracts, and both are eroded by sellers who use several platforms at once and by regulators, as Alibaba's "choose one from two" fine shows.

### Trust has always been hybrid, and the "private order" story is weak at both ends

Trust has always rested on reputation working inside public or publicly backed enforcement (moderate–high). The chain runs from Kanesh's written contracts and stated interest rates, through the aediles' disclosure rules, the *agoranomoi*, piepowder courts, Champagne's fair-wardens, the *muhtasib* and Kumasi's market queens, to eBay's feedback.

This matters for a specific reason. The cleanest historical precedent offered for platform self-governance is the reputational coalition and the private law merchant. That is exactly the part of the medieval record that recent archival work has most undermined, and Kadens names cyber-law scholars among those who relied on the myth. eBay's own private order operated "in the shadow of the public order", and courts now set the boundaries of what platforms may do.

Digital reputation is more systematic than bazaar gossip, but it has a problem that Geertz's clientelised bazaar did not: rating inflation. Resnick and Zeckhauser found eBay feedback "almost always positive" in 1999 data, which limits how much information it carries.

### The law of the marketplace has flipped from forestalling to anti-steering

For about five centuries, English law made dealing outside the open market a crime, so that the marketplace, with its tolls and price supervision, could not be bypassed. The 1844 Act ended that protection. Today's regulators prohibit the reverse. The DMA fines against Apple and Google Play, the Epic litigation and the CMA's steering consultations all forbid marketplaces from stopping traders telling buyers about cheaper offers elsewhere. Platforms, for their part, fight "disintermediation" (buyers and sellers completing repeat deals off-platform) through retention, as the NFX canon advises ([NFX](https://www.nfx.com/post/network-effects-bible)).

In short, the medieval legal system protected the marketplace against the off-market deal; the modern one protects the off-market deal against the marketplace (high on the facts; moderate on the interpretation). Price supervision has also returned, but in private hands. Temu's "price evaluation system" echoes the Athenian *sitophylakes* and the *muhtasib*, without their public mandate.

### Place gave way to process, but physical markets keep finding new jobs

The abstraction is real and can be dated. The dictionary sense of "market" as supply and demand appears in the 1680s; Chicago's grain receipts traded without the grain in the mid-nineteenth century; NASDAQ quotations went electronic in 1971; Big Bang came in 1986. Hayek's "one market" that nobody surveys as a whole is its theory. Yet every wave of abstraction left physical markets doing something else: Victorian halls served civic order, car boot sales thrift, farmers' markets provenance, and London's markets social mixing. Each new retail form took one function of the old market. Department stores took spectacle, mail order took reach, supermarkets took cheap food and malls took sociability. Platforms now take matching and trust (moderate; synthesis). Granovetter's rule of thumb explains why the physical market survives: markets become less *personal*, not less *social*.

### Closure and opening alternate, and rulers open markets when it pays

Incumbents repeatedly close markets: the Japanese *za*, merchant guilds, the Buttonwood club and fixed commissions, platform exclusivity. Rulers repeatedly force them open when the fiscal or political gain from open trade exceeds the rents from privilege. Examples are *rakuichi rakuza* from 1549, Kaifeng's legalised street shops, the 1844 repeal of the forestalling laws, the SEC reforms of 1975, Big Bang in 1986 and the DMA (moderate). Ogilvie's finding that guilds lasted as long as they paid both members and rulers is the best single explanation of why such openings come late.

### How far the historical parallels to digital platforms hold

| Historical institution | Digital analogue | Verdict | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sovereign market charter (three-quarters of English markets by royal grant) | Operator-built venue; later designation as gatekeeper (EU) or with strategic market status (UK) | Holds structurally, but legitimacy now flows from network effects, with the state arriving afterwards | Moderate–high |
| Tolls, stallage, customs | Take rates, fees, advertising | Holds in structure; levels not comparable | Moderate (structure); low (levels) |
| Distance rule (6⅔ miles), exclusion zones | Network effects, exclusivity, blocking rival agents | Partial: then a legal monopoly; now economic and contractual, eroded by multi-platform sellers | Moderate |
| Market days, rotating fairs | Constrained launches, atomic networks | Holds: the same thickness problem | Moderate |
| Fair-wardens, piepowder courts, *muhtasib* | Platform dispute resolution, guarantees, moderation | Holds functionally, but the historical versions were public or publicly backed | Moderate–high |
| Maghribi coalition and private law merchant | Feedback as a self-sufficient private order | Weak: the historical base is contested, and Kadens names its misuse in cyber-law | Moderate–high that the parallel is overstated |
| Forestalling offences | Platform anti-leakage tactics versus anti-steering law | Inverted: law once protected the market from off-market deals; now it protects off-market deals from the market | High (facts); moderate (reading) |
| State trading in its own market (Han monopolies, *Arthaśāstra* "single channel") | Amazon's own retail (about 40% of units) alongside third-party sellers | The conflict of interest is old; its private, global form is new | Low–moderate |
| SABRE's display bias (banned 1984) | Search self-preferencing (Google, €460m, 2026); Amazon Buy Box commitments | Direct precedent: the same conduct and the same remedy logic | High |
| Minitel kiosk revenue split | App-store commission | Close analogue | Moderate |
| Victorian municipalisation | Seller verification duties, deemed-supplier VAT, product-safety duties | Departure: the state once took ownership; now it deputises the private owner | Moderate |
| Feudal lordship | "Technofeudalism", "cloud rent" | Weak: platforms invest and compete like capitalist firms (Morozov), and putting-out fits platform labour better (Kenney & Zysman) | Low that it holds |

The parallels that hold best are structural: franchise, toll, catchment protection and publicly backed dispute resolution. One is a direct institutional precedent: SABRE's display bias, banned in 1984, is the same conduct for which Google was fined €460m in 2026. The parallels that hold worst are the ones most often used in polemic: technofeudalism, and the private reputational coalition as a model for platform self-regulation. Three features are genuinely new. The first is a single private operator governing hundreds of millions of participants; eBay alone reports 135m active buyers ([Value Added Resource](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/ebay-q4-2025-earnings/)). The second is allocation of attention by ranking algorithms. The third is a franchise that arises from network effects rather than a sovereign grant, which is why regulators now arrive after the fact to designate rather than beforehand to charter.

## Conclusion

The history of marketplaces is less a story of markets escaping government than of governance changing hands. The medieval system spread a marketplace's functions across many holders: the crown granted the right, the lord collected tolls, the borough or steward ran the court, the assize fixed the loaf, and the priory owned the fair. The nineteenth-century state pulled these functions together into municipal ownership. Platforms have pulled them together again, privately and globally, and added one that historically belonged to states such as Han China: trading in the market they govern. The current regulatory response has a distinctive shape. Rather than taking markets into public ownership, as the Victorian Parliament did, states are deputising the private owner as inspector, tax collector and safety officer, while forbidding it to monopolise the buyer. If the long record is a guide, expect more layering of public duties and periodic forced openings, in the line of *rakuichi rakuza* and Big Bang, rather than either laissez-faire or break-up (moderate).

For marketplace builders the record offers three non-obvious lessons. First, failure is the base rate. Of English places holding a market or a grant in 1300, only 39% were market towns three centuries later. The modern playbook, built from survivors, has no comparable count of failures. Second, trust infrastructure has never been purely private. Designing for regulators as co-governors, through verification, disclosure and routes to escalate disputes, is the historically normal condition, not a tax on innovation. Third, the oldest lever is control of where buyers and sellers meet: Bracton's catchment, the forestalling laws, the Buy Box. If the Ninth Circuit's view that a shopping agent is simply the user's tool survives, AI agents become something close to lawful forestallers: intermediaries that reach the buyer before the marketplace does. Take rates will then have to be justified against that bypass (low–moderate on the trajectory; high on the legal facts to date).
